3.1 Tooling & Materials 
●​ Frekote: Semi-permanent release agent. Applied in thin coats; mirrors tool surface.

12.​ Technical Case Study: “Explain how you would 
design a mold for a laminated part” 
This is the specific question a previous Joby candidate received. Don't just list steps; explain the 
engineering logic. 
Step 1: Material Selection (CTE Match) 
●​ Question: Why choose one tool material over another? 
●​ Answer: "I first consider the CTE (Coefficient of Thermal Expansion). If I'm curing a 
carbon fiber part at 250°F, an Aluminum tool will expand significantly more than the part. 
Upon cooling, the tool shrinks more than the part, inducing internal stresses or warping. 
For aerospace tolerances, I would design a Carbon Fiber Tool 
(Composite-to-Composite) to ensure the CTE is matched, or use Invar for extremely 
high-volume production." 
Step 2: Master Pattern & Flanges 
●​ Logic: You need a "Plug" to pull the "Mold." 
●​ Detail: "I would CNC machine a master pattern from a high-density tooling board (like 
RenShape). I’d ensure the flanges are wide enough (at least 2-3 inches) to provide a 
stable surface for the Tacky Tape and vacuum seal, and to prevent the bag from 'pulling' 
on the part edges." 
Step 3: Surface Finish & Release 
●​ Logic: The part is only as smooth as the tool. 
●​ Detail: "The pattern would be sealed with a high-temp sealer (like Duratec) and sanded 
to 2000 grit. I’d apply a semi-permanent release agent like Frekote. I’d choose Frekote 
over wax because it’s a 'chemical' release that doesn't build up in corners, preserving the 
aerodynamic profile of the airfoil." 
Step 4: Air-Pathing & Vacuum Integrity 
●​ Logic: You can't have a vacuum if air is trapped. 
●​ Detail: "I’d design the mold with integrated vacuum ports or ensures 'breather' paths 
lead to every deep pocket. For a complex Joby part, I’d consider a Split Mold 
(Clamshell) with indexed pins to ensure the two halves align perfectly for a seamless 
leading edge." 
13.​ Technical Intuition: Comparison Guide 
Aluminum vs. Carbon Fiber Stress-Strain Curves (SpaceX Question) 
 

●​ Aluminum: Ductile. It has a linear elastic region, a yield point, and then a long plastic 
deformation region before failure. It "bends before it breaks." 
●​ Carbon Fiber: Brittle and Anisotropic. It is linear elastic nearly all the way to failure. It 
does not "yield"; it fails catastrophically once the fibers snap or the matrix cracks. 
●​ The "Intuition": Aluminum is predictable and isotropic (same properties in all 
directions). Carbon is a "designed material"—you can make it 10x stronger in one 
direction than the other, which is why it's perfect for Joby's weight-sensitive rotors. 
The "Lathe Cube" Logic (Skyroot Question) 
●​ Question: How to cut a 50x50 cube from a cylinder on a Lathe? 
●​ The Trick: Lathes rotate parts. You can't easily cut a "cube" purely on a lathe without a 
4-jaw chuck and offset turning (turning one face at a time). 
●​ The "Manufacturing Mindset": "While I could use a 4-jaw chuck to offset and turn four 
flat faces, a more efficient manufacturing approach would be to face/center-drill the 
cylinder on the lathe, then move it to a 3-axis Mill using a vice to cut the square profile."

14.​ High-level resources for Aerospace Manufacturing 
To move beyond the hobbyist level, check these out: 
1.​ SAMPE (Society for the Advancement of Material and Process Engineering): Their 
"Technical Papers" are the gold standard for what SpaceX and Joby actually do. 
2.​ CompositesWorld (Website/Magazine): Look for their "High-Rate Production" and 
"Automated Fiber Placement (AFP)" articles. 
3.​ YouTube - "The Efficient Engineer": Watch his videos on Stress Tensors and 
Composite Mechanics. 
4.​ Book: Manufacturing Processes for Advanced Composites by F.C. Campbell. This is the 
"Bible" for aerospace manufacturing engineers. 
5.​ NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS): Search for "Composite Delamination" or 
"Autoclave Processing." It’s free and is the exact data Joby engineers look at.
